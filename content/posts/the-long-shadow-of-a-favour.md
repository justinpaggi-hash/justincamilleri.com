Referrals are a normal part of business.

A trusted contact may recommend someone for a vacancy. Such recommendations can be valuable, introducing an organisation to candidates who might otherwise never have been considered.

The difficulty starts when a referral feels less like a recommendation and more like an obligation.

This can happen because of past favours, longstanding family or business relationships, or because the person making the recommendation has influence over the person responsible for recruitment.

Nobody necessarily needs to say, "Employ this person."

Sometimes the relationship itself creates the expectation.

That is when a simple favour can become a wider management problem.

## A referral should open the door, not bypass it

There is an important difference between referring someone and expecting someone to be employed.

A referral should create an opportunity to be considered. It should not remove the need to establish whether the candidate is suitable for the role.

The problem starts when the normal recruitment process is bypassed or weakened. There may be no competitive selection, certain stages may be skipped, or a process may formally take place while those involved already understand the expected outcome.

This does not mean the person recruited is unsuitable. They may be highly capable and eventually become one of the strongest members of the team.

The issue is not necessarily the person. It is the process through which that person entered the organisation and the perceptions that process can create.

## When a recommendation is difficult to refuse

The situation becomes more complex when the person making the recommendation has influence over the person receiving it.

A board member may recommend someone to an executive. A shareholder may approach management about a vacancy. A senior figure may suggest someone for a role within an organisation that falls within their sphere of influence.

No explicit instruction or threat is necessary.

When the person making the recommendation has influence over the decision maker's mandate, resources or professional future, even a polite suggestion can carry considerable weight.

The decision maker may technically remain free to refuse while still having to consider what refusing could mean.

This creates a difficult question:

**Is the person being recommended for consideration, or is there an expectation that the person will be employed?**

The harder that question is to answer, the more important an independent recruitment process becomes.

## The consequences begin after recruitment

People can respond very differently to being recruited through a relationship.

Some may feel a greater responsibility to prove themselves. Someone has placed their reputation behind them, and they may work harder to demonstrate that the confidence was justified.

Others may perceive that the relationship which secured their position also provides protection within the organisation. They may believe that management will find it difficult to challenge them because they retain access to the person who facilitated their appointment.

They may begin to feel untouchable.

But they do not actually need to feel untouchable for the problem to emerge.

It may be enough for their colleagues to believe that they are.

Once that perception exists, ordinary management decisions can take on a different meaning. An attractive assignment may be seen as preferential treatment. A decision not to discipline may be interpreted as protection. Praise may be questioned. Promotion may be attributed to the original relationship rather than performance.

The question gradually becomes:

**Do the same rules apply to everyone?**

At that point, the recruitment decision is no longer about one employee. It has started affecting trust within the team.

## When good performance is not enough

One of the more difficult consequences arises when the referred employee performs exceptionally well.

They may work hard, deliver strong results and genuinely deserve recognition or promotion. Yet the circumstances of their recruitment may continue to follow them.

A deserved promotion can still be interpreted as another favour. Recognition can be discounted and opportunities attributed to connections rather than competence.

This creates an unfair burden on the employee.

Management can also become trapped. Promoting a deserving employee may reinforce perceptions of favouritism, while holding that person back because of those perceptions creates a different form of unfairness.

The favour intended to help someone can therefore make it harder for that person to establish that later achievements were earned.

## Management can become constrained too

The opposite situation creates another problem.

If the referred employee underperforms, can management deal with the situation in exactly the same way as it would with anyone else?

Issuing a warning, refusing an opportunity or eventually ending the employment relationship may become more difficult when the original referrer remains influential.

This is particularly problematic when that person also has influence over the manager's own position.

There is a further complication. Employees usually see the manager who appointed and manages the individual. They do not necessarily see the circumstances surrounding the recruitment.

Management can therefore become associated with favouritism even where the original pressure came from elsewhere.

A decision influenced from above can become a leadership problem below.

## The process protects everyone

The answer is not to eliminate referrals.

A referral can provide useful information about a candidate, particularly where the person making it has direct knowledge of their capabilities. Personal and professional networks are also an unavoidable reality in many organisations and family businesses.

The distinction is between a referral that creates an opportunity and a referral that determines an outcome**.

A referred candidate can still be assessed against the requirements of the role and, where appropriate, compared with other candidates.

Once recruited, the same principle should continue. Performance, recognition, progression and accountability should be based on the same standards applied to comparable employees.

These safeguards become more important as the influence of the person making the referral increases.

They protect the organisation and management, but they also protect the referred employee by giving credibility to the appointment and to what that person achieves afterwards.

## The long shadow

A favour during recruitment may appear to be a small decision. Its consequences can last much longer.

A poor performer may be perceived as protected. A strong performer may struggle to have success recognised on its own merits. Management may find its authority constrained, while colleagues begin questioning whether the same standards apply to everyone.

The greatest risk of favour based recruitment is therefore not simply employing the wrong person.

It is creating uncertainty about whether merit, performance and accountability still determine what happens next.

A referral should create an opportunity, not guarantee an outcome.

Once someone walks through the door, what follows should depend on what that person does, not on who opened it for them.
