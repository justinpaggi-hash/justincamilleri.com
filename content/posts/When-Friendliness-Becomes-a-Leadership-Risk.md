Leadership is often associated with approachability. We are encouraged to build relationships, earn trust, and create environments where people feel comfortable sharing ideas and concerns. These are all important qualities.

However, there is a point where friendliness, if left unchecked, begins to work against the very objectives it was intended to achieve.

The issue is not friendliness itself. The issue is the gradual erosion of professional boundaries.

Professional boundaries define the relationship between a leader and those around them. They preserve objectivity, clarify expectations, and ensure that decisions are guided by organisational priorities rather than personal relationships.

Without these boundaries, leaders risk making decisions based on who people are rather than on what the organisation needs.

## Clients and Stakeholders

Strong relationships with clients and stakeholders are essential. They facilitate cooperation, improve communication, and help resolve challenges.

Yet relationships should never replace professional judgement.

When leaders become overly familiar with external stakeholders, there is a risk that preserving the relationship becomes more important than protecting the organisation's interests. Concessions become easier to justify. Difficult conversations are postponed. Requests receive favourable treatment because of personal rapport rather than strategic merit.

Over time, the organisation's priorities can gradually become secondary.

The strongest stakeholder relationships are not those built on personal favour, but on mutual trust, credibility, and professionalism.

## Leading Internal Teams

The same principle applies within organisations.

Employees generally appreciate leaders who are approachable and supportive. These qualities contribute to psychological safety and open communication. However, when professional boundaries become unclear, role clarity begins to weaken.

A leader may hesitate to address poor performance, avoid difficult conversations, or make exceptions that would not be granted to others. Team members may become uncertain about expectations or begin to perceive inconsistent treatment.

The challenge is rarely excessive friendliness.

The challenge is inconsistency.

When standards depend on personal relationships, fairness is questioned. Once fairness is questioned, credibility begins to decline.

## Why Consistency Matters

People rarely judge leaders on isolated decisions. They judge them on patterns.

Consistency allows employees, clients, and stakeholders to understand what to expect. Similar situations are treated in similar ways. Decisions become predictable. Expectations become clearer.

This does not mean leaders should become rigid.

Circumstances change, and leadership requires judgement. Different situations sometimes require different decisions. However, when leaders depart from established practice, they should be able to explain why. Transparency protects credibility.

Consistency is therefore not the opposite of flexibility.

It is the foundation that makes flexibility acceptable.

## Finding the Balance

Maintaining professional boundaries does not require becoming distant or impersonal.

It requires deliberate discipline.

Leaders can strengthen this balance by following a few simple principles.

- Define expectations early and apply them consistently.
- Separate personal relationships from professional decisions.
- Address difficult conversations promptly instead of avoiding them.
- Ensure that decisions can be explained using objective criteria rather than personal preference.
- Reflect regularly on whether exceptions are becoming the norm rather than the exception.

These practices help preserve fairness while maintaining positive working relationships.

## Final Reflection

Leadership is not about choosing between being approachable and being authoritative.

It is about understanding that both qualities are necessary.

Approachability encourages trust.

Professional boundaries protect objectivity.

Consistency builds credibility.

The most effective leaders recognise that relationships are an important part of leadership, but they should never become the basis for leadership decisions. People may value leaders who are friendly, but they respect leaders who are fair, consistent, and willing to make difficult decisions when the organisation requires them.
